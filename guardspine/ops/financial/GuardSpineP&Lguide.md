Michaela Lehr (VP Finance at ActionIQ) walks senior operators through how SaaS companies are judged through the P&L lens, and why leaders outside finance should understand the board/investor “mental model.” She frames startup maturity as: first find product–market fit, then iterate on monetization/go-to-market using a tight set of financial and operating metrics. She distinguishes GAAP (audited, rule-governed reporting—where the P&L lives) from non-GAAP (operating/board metrics like ARR, churn, CAC payback, LTV:CAC). She then decomposes the SaaS P&L (revenue → COGS → gross margin → operating expenses → EBITDA) and explains how boards interpret growth, margin, and efficiency together (e.g., rule of 40). Finally, she links the P&L to the “queen” operating metrics—ARR and LTV:CAC—and highlights practical realities: revenue recognition depends on delivering value (performance obligations), customer-facing costs create classification tradeoffs (COGS vs S&M), multi-year/termination clauses can disqualify ARR, and consumption pricing often gets “ARR-like” treatment via rolling usage baselines.

Why she’s teaching this (operator motive)

Boards/investors assess businesses through a small set of financial primitives; leaders are more effective if they can tie plans to top line, bottom line, and the metrics in between.

The goal isn’t “finance theater”; it’s to run the org to maximize value (growth that can be underwritten + margins that fund the machine).

Startup phase framing

Early: validate PMF (“do people buy and get value?”).

After PMF: monetize and scale via iterative GTM—this is where the metrics become non-optional.

GAAP vs non-GAAP (what gets audited vs what gets operated)

GAAP: governed by accounting standards; auditors care (e.g., revenue recognition, gross margin).

Non-GAAP: used to run the business and evaluated by boards/investors, but not audited (ARR, churn, retention, CAC, free cash flow, etc.).

Many non-GAAP metrics are derived from GAAP numbers but are operationally “cleaner.”

Three financial statements (table stakes literacy)

Balance sheet: point-in-time snapshot of assets vs liabilities/equity (“what is the business worth today?”).

Income statement / P&L: performance over time (monthly/quarterly/annual).

Cash flow statement: cash movement across operations/investing/financing; tightly linked to balance sheet changes.

P&L anatomy for SaaS (and what each line “means”)

Revenue (top line): often split into subscription/platform vs services.

Key mechanic: revenue recognition is tied to delivering on performance obligations (ASC 606). Faster implementations can accelerate recognition because customers can start deriving value sooner.

Practical note: there’s “art” in defining when value delivery begins (CFO + auditors manage that boundary).

COGS: cost to operate/deliver the product (cloud infrastructure, certain customer-facing delivery work, etc.).

Why it matters: it drives gross margin, a primary indicator of product monetizability (and a central input to unit economics).

Operating expenses (below the line / Opex):

S&M, R&D, G&A—boards watch these as a % of revenue and as efficiency levers.

She cites common heuristic bands (varies by business): S&M ~40–50%, R&D ~30–40%, G&A ~≤20% (with the usual board bias that “less G&A is better”).

Net income vs EBITDA:

Net income includes “non-core” items (interest, taxes, depreciation/amortization, etc.).

Boards often focus on EBITDA as the “operational bottom line.”

How boards “read” a SaaS P&L

Growth (often framed as ARR growth rather than GAAP revenue growth).

Gross margin (because it funds everything else and feeds unit economics like LTV and CAC payback).

Opex efficiency (spend translating into predictable growth).

Rule of 40: growth % + EBITDA margin % as a quick health heuristic (with current market nuance: some argue for cash-based variants or lower thresholds during profitability transitions).

Revenue vs bookings (sales leadership confusion she addresses)

Boards sometimes push sales to focus on bookings as a leading indicator.

But revenue recognition depends on delivery/implementation—so bookings aren’t “the whole story,” just the forward signal.

The “geography dance” (classification tradeoffs that affect metrics)

Where teams sit on the P&L changes how metrics look:

Putting more customer-facing cost in COGS can hurt gross margin.

Putting more in S&M can inflate CAC and worsen CAC payback.

Auditors can allow splits (e.g., tracked customer-facing hours in COGS, other work below the line), but they resist overly aggressive reclassifications.

Non-GAAP operating stack (what she treats as core)

ARR / MRR (the “queen”): operationally clean top-line proxy; boards often mean ARR when they say “top line.”

ASP / ACV: indicates market segment (enterprise vs mid-market vs SMB) and must cohere with GTM claims.

Pipeline coverage: investors want repeatability; typical heuristic is multiple-X coverage vs near-term quota/plan.

Retention & churn:

Gross churn: what dollars fall out.

Net retention / net churn: churn netted with expansion (upsells); crucial for revenue “quality” and valuation underwriting.

She stresses: churn/retention is an everyone problem (not just CS), because it reflects ICP fit, product reality, and delivery.

CAC payback: time to recoup acquisition cost using subscription revenue times gross margin; common target ≈ 12 months (sometimes longer tolerated in enterprise motions).

LTV:CAC (the “queen of queens”): compresses multiple levers—pricing, retention, expansion, margin, and acquisition efficiency.

Baseline heuristic she gives: ~3× is viability, 3–5× is strong, >5× is excellent.

Reality: every investor calculates it slightly differently, so know the baseline before you “narrate.”

Contract terms that break ARR (and why finance pushes back)

Trials: often don’t count as ARR because they’re not recurring by nature (even if GAAP revenue can exist).

Termination for convenience: typically disqualifies ARR because the revenue isn’t truly “committed recurring.”

Termination for cause: more gray; risk tolerance varies, but the conservative stance is to exclude or heavily caveat.

Consumption pricing (ARR retrofit)

She notes a common industry adaptation: define “recurring” as a rolling baseline (e.g., last 3 months of usage) to create an ARR-like run-rate narrative—useful, but fundamentally different from contracted recurring revenue.

(b) Leading indicators mapped to each P&L line

1. Revenue (GAAP) — “recognizable value delivered”
   Leading indicators

Implementation velocity: TTFV, go-live rate, backlog age

Product usage activation within 30 days of close

Contract terms that delay recognition (milestone-based acceptance, heavy contingencies)

Billing readiness: invoicing timeliness, customer procurement delays
Interpretation

GAAP revenue is downstream of delivery. If onboarding stalls, revenue lags even with strong bookings.

2. ARR (non-GAAP top line) — “committed recurring base”
   Leading indicators

Qualified bookings that are ARR-eligible (no termination-for-convenience/trial ambiguity)

Renewal pipeline coverage for next 2 quarters

Expansion signals: seat growth, usage growth, new use-case adoption
Interpretation

ARR is “board top line.” Contract structure can disqualify ARR even when sales celebrates.

3. COGS — “cost to run and deliver the product”
   Leading indicators

Cloud unit cost (cost per active customer / per event / per GB / per API call—pick your unit)

Support load: tickets per customer, severity, hours per ticket

Services delivery hours per implementation and rework rate

Reliability: incidents, latency regressions (they drive support and churn)
Interpretation

Gross margin problems often start as unit-cost creep + support burden, not “finance noise.”

4. Gross margin — “fuel available to fund growth”
   Leading indicators

Unit economics by segment (enterprise customers often cost more to serve)

Services attach rate and services margin trend

Product architecture choices impacting infra cost curves
Interpretation

Gross margin is the silent multiplier inside CAC payback and LTV; it’s a valuation governor.

5. Sales & Marketing (S&M) — “cost to create and close demand”
   Leading indicators

Pipeline creation efficiency ($ pipeline per $ spend)

Win rate and sales cycle time (ramp issues show here first)

Discount depth and non-standard terms frequency (quality decay)

Rep productivity distribution (median vs top-quartile; bench strength)
Interpretation

S&M spend can be “right” even when CAC looks temporarily bad—if ramp timing is explained and tracked.

6. R&D — “rate of product capability creation”
   Leading indicators

Roadmap execution: shipped-to-planned, cycle time, defect escape rate

Reliability and performance improvements (feeds COGS + retention)

Customer-request closure rate for top renewal drivers
Interpretation

Under-investing in R&D can improve EBITDA short term while detonating retention later.

7. G&A — “org overhead and control systems”
   Leading indicators

Close process health (days to close, forecast accuracy)

Compliance/security readiness for enterprise procurement

Billing/collections performance (invoicing speed, DSO)
Interpretation

“Lean G&A” is good until it breaks controls, slows collections, or creates audit surprises.

8. EBITDA / operating margin — “operational profitability”
   Leading indicators

Margin trend (gross margin, opex ratios)

Operating leverage: revenue growth vs headcount growth

One-time items tracked separately with policy consistency
Interpretation

Boards tolerate losses if the path to leverage is coherent and consistently measured.

(c) Top 10 failure modes that cause board-level trust loss (even when growth looks good)
Metric definition drift

ARR, churn, CAC, or payback changes without a bridge explaining “old vs new” and why.

Bookings celebrated, revenue/ARR disappoints—no operational bridge

Weak implementation velocity, acceptance contingencies, or term issues… but leadership acts surprised.

Retention weakness rationalized as “CS problem”

No ICP/sales-quality/product-value root cause analysis; no cross-functional owner.

Gross margin erosion hidden behind growth

Cloud costs and support burden rising faster than revenue; unit costs not instrumented.

Reclassifications used as camouflage

Moving costs between COGS/S&M/G&A to “fix” metrics without changing drivers; auditors later force reversals.

Pipeline optics without pipeline integrity

Coverage looks fine but is inflated by stage gaming, stale deals, or low-quality opportunities; forecast misses repeat.

CAC payback deterioration with no credible ramp narrative

Hiring spree without clear productivity milestones, rep enablement plan, or time-to-productivity tracking.

Discounting and contract concessions as hidden churn

Growth purchased via deep discounts, termination convenience, excessive services, or roadmap promises that later hit retention.

Cash surprises

Collections slip, DSO spikes, big customer non-payment, or burn jumps—without early warning indicators and mitigation.

“One-time” becomes habitual

Repeated restructuring/adjustments treated as non-operational every quarter; board concludes the core model isn’t stable.
